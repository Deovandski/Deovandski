One does not simply *look* at these photographs; one is, rather, gently but insistently looked back at by them. Here is an artist who arrived at the medium not through the cloistered halls of the academy but by the far nobler road of trial and error (failing, learning, failing anew), a pedagogy he shares, one notes with no small delight, with Peter Lik, that self-taught Australian colossus of the supersaturated panorama.

Observe, first, the fog. In *Headlights in the fog*, two pale suns approach through a grey so total it ceases to be weather and becomes metaphysics. The motif returns, transfigured, in *Godrays at the concert*, where shafts of stage light cleave the smoke like the fingers of some benevolent and possibly ticketed deity, and again in *Ballet through the haze*, where the dancers do not so much dance as condense.

Then, the water. Our artist cannot pass a wet surface without interrogating it. *Rainy night neon reflections* and *Raindrops on the window* converse across the years with Saul Leiter, poet of the rain-streaked pane, who likewise understood that the most honest view of a city is the one slightly obscured.

Consider, too, his geometry of depth. *Long shadows on the walkway*, *Bamboo walkway*, *Quiet side street*: each is a corridor drawn with the widest lens at hand, tugging the eye toward a vanishing point it can never quite reach, which is, of course, the human condition, rendered at roughly eighty-two degrees of field of view.

And yet he is no mere architect of distance. *Lonely arches* sets a fast-food sign against an empty sky with all the solitude of an Edward Hopper canvas and none of the overtime. *Footprint and a sprout*, meanwhile, offers the entire cycle of life, death and the beach in a single frame.

His palette refuses moderation. The reds are redder (*Red maple*), the blues bluer (*Blue trees and red dirt*), the contrasts punchier, as though the world, sensing it was about to be captured, had put on its very best colours for the occasion.

What unites it all is the moment: that exact angle, that exact instant, seen once and never again. One could say much more. One has, in fact, already said rather too much. But such is the price of beauty: it obliges the critic to <span lang="pt" translate="no">encher linguiça</span>.
